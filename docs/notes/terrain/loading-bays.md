# Loading bays on an industrial site (#528, #496 phase 2)

The last piece of #496. #498 made a door's leaf a property of the door, #497 gave the sites their
insides, #516 their yards; this puts a row of roll-up bays on the wall between the two.

- **`DoorBudget.Bays(site, runLength, eave)`** is the run descriptor — count, centres, width,
  height — per site type. A haulier's are the widest and tallest in the game (4.5 m square: a 4 m
  box body has to clear the lintel), a works' 4.0 m at 4.5 m centres, a body shop's 3.2 m, and a
  **dealership gets none**: a showroom's front is glazed and a forecourt is walked across, not
  reversed into. The opening is capped by the wall it is cut in, and a wall too low for a vehicle
  door gets **none rather than a squashed one**.
- **Placement** is in `BuildingFootprint.Compute`, before the generic street-front rule and
  claiming the budget first, on the wall the **main door** is on — which is the wall `SiteYards`
  lays the yard in front of (#516), so the bays face their own fleet and not the back hedge.
- **`Hang = RollUp, Vehicle = true` is the whole of it.** `DoorLeaf`, `InteriorManager.OpenForVehicle`,
  `DoorIndex.VehicleDoorAhead`, the server's `ServerVehicleDoorReach` and the facade's baked slats
  all read the door rather than its building's kind, so nothing else needed changing. That is
  #498's design paying off.
- **A showroom's front door is no longer a shutter.** A dealership is GKLAS 1242 and so
  `BuildingKind.Garage`, whose main door defaults to roll-up — right for a workshop, wrong for the
  one site type whose front wall is meant to be looked through. Overridden per door.
- **The sign belongs to the main door.** `BuildingMeshBuilder.AppendDoor` hung the workshop-blue
  lit board over every roll-up door; four of them along a warehouse wall read as a parade of
  garages. A bay gets a painted lintel band instead, which is what marks one in a real yard.

## Three things the first cut got wrong

All three were found by the checks, none by reading the code back.

1. **`TooClose` rejected every bay after the first.** It enforces `DoorBudget.MinGap` (3 m) edge to
   edge, and bays at 4.5 m centres are 0.5 m apart *on purpose*. A bay's minimum is `BayRun.Pier`,
   the strip of wall carrying the two lintels — and it needs an epsilon under `Spacing - Width`, or
   neighbours at exactly the spacing fail the float compare.
2. **Bays centred on the wall straddle the main door**, which keeps its own `MinGap`, so the middle
   ones were rejected and the survivors ended up three spacings apart. They go **beside** the door
   now, and on **both sides**: taking only the longer side cost a 26 x 18 m depot half the bays its
   wall had room for.
3. **A pedestrian door's 3 m costs a body shop every bay it has room for.** `BayToDoorGap` is
   1.2 m — a works' office door stands right beside its first bay, which is also how one looks.

## `--doorcheck` encoded the rule this changes

#498's check asserted that *every* extra door is a plain pedestrian door and that *every* pair of
doors is `MinGap` apart. Both are exactly what a loading bay breaks, so it failed with eight
findings the moment bays appeared — correctly. It now allows a wide, roll-up, driven-through extra
door **only on a building `BuildingTypes.SiteFor` calls a site**, and allows a pier between two
bays and `BayToDoorGap` between a bay and the main door, while every other pair still keeps
`MinGap`. An ordinary building's rules are untouched.

## A turned solid, and what it does and does not buy

`--sitecheck`'s warehouse is turned **31°** (#524's lesson). Every real Swiss building is at some
angle and every synthetic one here was square to the world, so the rotated path — bay placement on
an angled wall, `DoorOnWall` against angled triangles, `FitEntry` and `Entrances` in a turned plan
frame, and the validator's entrance-to-doorway match — was not exercised at all. It is now, and
the turned warehouse keeps its 4 bays and a valid plan.

It does **not** catch a mirrored wall tangent, and that was checked rather than assumed: inverting
`t` in the bay loop leaves `--sitecheck` and `--doorcheck` both green. The reason is not a weak
test — it is that **a bay row is symmetric about the wall's middle by construction**, so a mirror
maps the set of positions onto itself. Where the row would be asymmetric (a wall with room on only
one side), a mirror puts the bays where there is no wall and `DoorOnWall` drops them, which the
count assertion does see. So the handedness of `t` is genuinely immaterial here — unlike
`SiteYards`'s heading (#516), where it decides which way a vehicle faces and a physical
nose-before-tail assertion was needed. The general rule this is an instance of — *ask whether the
thing under test is asymmetric before adding a turned case, and say so when it is not* — is in
`several-doors` (#524).

## Check

`--doorcheck` (tier 1) covers it, and its 60 x 30 m works hall now goes through the industrial
generator: 5 doors, 4 of them bays, each arriving at a doorway inside with the inside and outside
agreeing on how it hangs. `--sitecheck` asserts the count per trade, all bays on one wall and the
same width, each neighbouring pair either a pier apart or with the main door between them, and no
bay taller than its wall; windowed it writes `<shot>_bays.png` across a warehouse front.
`LoadingBayTests` pins the arithmetic in tier 0 (12 tests).

**Not covered, and worth knowing:** there is no check of a vehicle actually *driving through* a
bay, on one peer or two. `--garagecheck` does that for a garage, but it needs a real building in a
real world, and **no industrial building exists in the generated world or any fixture** — which is
why `--sitecheck` builds its own. Until one does, the drive-through and its remote-peer behaviour
are unverified. See the follow-up issue.
